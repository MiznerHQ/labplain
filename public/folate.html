What does your
Folate result mean?

Vitamins
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

A folate test measures the amount of folate (Vitamin B9) in your blood. Folate is an essential nutrient that your body needs to make red and white blood cells, repair tissues, and build DNA. Because your body cannot store large amounts of folate, you rely on a steady supply from foods like leafy greens, citrus fruits, beans, and fortified grains.

Folate works closely with Vitamin B12 to keep your nervous system healthy and to make sure your red blood cells develop normally. When folate levels drop too low, your body cannot produce enough healthy red blood cells, which can look like a condition called megaloblastic anemia.

Doctors frequently order this test alongside Vitamin B12 if you are experiencing unexplained fatigue, nerve tingling, or changes in your mood, or to ensure proper nutrition during pregnancy, when folate requirements increase significantly.

Normal reference range
Normal Range: 2.7–17.0 ng/mL

A standard blood test measures serum folate, which reflects your recent dietary intake. Because of this, eating a high-folate meal right before a test can temporarily shift your numbers.

Some medical practices look at red blood cell (RBC) folate instead, which measures folate stored inside cells over the long term. Reference ranges always vary slightly between testing facilities, so make sure to check the specific normal range printed on your lab report.

⏰ Why preparation matters

Your doctor may ask you to fast (avoiding food and drink except water) for several hours before a serum folate test. This prevents recent meals or supplements from artificially inflating your results, giving your medical team a clearer view of your true baseline levels.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

Elevated folate levels are most often associated with high-dose vitamin supplementation or fortified food intake. High blood levels can also occasionally reflect an underlying Vitamin B12 deficiency, as the body requires B12 to properly process and use folate inside cells.

↓ If Low

Low folate values frequently indicate a folate deficiency. This may reflect an inadequate dietary intake, poor absorption in the digestive tract, chronic alcohol consumption, or a period of increased physical demand, such as pregnancy.

Symptoms associated with abnormal folate

↑ High Folate Symptoms

  • Usually causes no noticeable physical symptoms on its own
  • May mask symptoms of an underlying Vitamin B12 deficiency
  • Can potentially hide neurological shifts caused by low B12

↓ Low Folate Symptoms

  • Persistent fatigue and muscle weakness
  • Pale skin or a slightly yellowish tint
  • Shortness of breath or dizziness
  • Sore mouth, tongue swelling, or mouth ulcers
  • Irritability or mood fluctuations
  • Pins-and-needles or tingling sensations
  • Difficulty concentrating or mild confusion
Common causes of abnormal folate

What can raise folate?

The primary cause of high folate levels is taking supplements or multivitamins containing folic acid. Because many grains, breads, and cereals are heavily fortified with folic acid, a diet rich in processed foods can also lead to higher blood values. Less commonly, an undetected Vitamin B12 deficiency can allow folate to build up in the bloodstream because the body needs B12 to actively pull folate into its tissues.

What can lower folate?

Low folate can stem from a diet that lacks fresh fruits, vegetables, and fortified foods. It can also be associated with malabsorption issues—where the small intestine struggles to absorb nutrients—which is frequently seen in conditions like celiac disease or Crohn's disease. Heavy alcohol use interferes with how your liver handles and stores folate while speeding up how quickly your kidneys filter it out. Additionally, during pregnancy or periods of tissue healing, the body's demand for folate rises sharply, which can drain your reserves if they are not actively replaced.

Tests often ordered alongside folate

Folate is best understood when looked at alongside other nutritional and blood markers. Your doctor may check:

  • Vitamin B12 — ordered because deficiencies in B12 and folate share identical anemia symptoms but require entirely different treatments
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC) — looks at red blood cell sizes; unusually large cells (macrocytosis) often hint at folate or B12 issues
  • RBC Folate — measures the folate inside your red blood cells to check your long-term nutritional status
  • Homocysteine — an amino acid that often rises when your body is low on folate or Vitamin B12
  • Iron and Ferritin — evaluates your overall iron storage to rule out other common reasons for fatigue or anemia
What to do next
An isolated folate level provides just one piece of your health puzzle. Because folate levels change in response to recent meals and can interact with other vitamins like B12, your doctor will evaluate your results alongside your daily diet, current symptoms, and full blood panel. Do not begin high-dose vitamin protocols on your own without checking with a professional, as balancing B-vitamins requires a coordinated approach.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Does my folate result explain the fatigue or symptoms I have been experiencing?
  • 02Were my Vitamin B12 levels checked at the same time, and how do they look compared to this?
  • 03Could any of my current medications, supplements, or dietary habits be throwing off my result?
  • 04Do you think my results point to an issue with what I am eating, or could my body be having trouble absorbing nutrients?
  • 05Would you recommend adding a specific supplement or adjusting my diet based on these numbers?
  • 06Do I need a follow-up test or a more specialized test like an RBC folate panel?
Frequently asked questions
What is the difference between folate and folic acid?
Folate is the natural form of Vitamin B9 found naturally in whole foods like spinach, asparagus, and lentils. Folic acid is the synthetic form of the vitamin used in manufactured supplements and added to fortified foods like cereal and flour. Your body processes both to fulfill its B9 needs.
Can a high folate level be dangerous?
High folate itself is generally considered non-toxic because it is water-soluble, meaning excess amounts usually pass through your urine. However, high folate can mask an unrecognized Vitamin B12 deficiency by correcting anemia symptoms while allowing nerve damage from low B12 to quietly progress.
How long does it take to fix low folate levels?
If a deficiency is present, dietary changes or doctor-recommended supplementation can often begin to normalize blood levels within a few weeks. However, fully rebuilding your body's red blood cells and tissue stores can take a few months.
Why is folate testing so important during pregnancy?
Folate is vital for early fetal development, specifically for the proper closing of the neural tube, which forms the brain and spinal cord. Ensuring adequate folate levels before and during pregnancy can help reduce the risk of congenital neural tube differences.
Can drinking alcohol affect my folate test?
Yes. Regular or heavy alcohol consumption interferes directly with how your body absorbs folate from food, compromises how your liver stores it, and increases how much folate your body excretes. This makes alcohol a common contributor to lower folate levels.
