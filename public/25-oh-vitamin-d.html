What does your
25-OH Vitamin D result mean?

Vitamins & Nutrition

Written by the LabPlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated July 2026

⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

The 25-hydroxyvitamin D test (often called the 25-OH vitamin D test) is the standard way to check the total amount of vitamin D in your body. Vitamin D enters your system through certain foods, supplements, and skin exposure to sunlight. Once absorbed, your liver processes it into 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the stable form that circulates in your blood and is what this test actually measures.

Vitamin D plays an important role in helping your body absorb calcium and phosphorus, which are essential for building and maintaining strong bones and teeth. It is also involved in supporting immune system function, regulating cellular growth, and managing inflammation.

Healthcare providers frequently order this test to evaluate bone health, investigate symptoms like chronic fatigue or bone pain, monitor individuals with malabsorption disorders, or check for overall nutritional deficiencies.

Normal reference range
Optimal Range: 30–100 ng/mL  |  Insufficient: 20–29 ng/mL

A level below 20 ng/mL is generally considered a vitamin D deficiency by mainstream clinical guidelines. Levels between 20 and 29 ng/mL are classified as insufficient, meaning your reserves may be lower than ideal.

Reference ranges can vary slightly depending on the lab and the units used (ng/mL vs. nmol/L). Always check the exact reference interval printed on your own lab report.

⏰ No Fasting Required

You don't need to fast before this test — you can eat and drink normally beforehand. It's commonly drawn alongside other routine bloodwork like a metabolic panel. Keep in mind levels also shift seasonally: they tend to run lower at the end of winter and peak in late summer, which your provider may factor into how they read your result.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

Elevated levels are almost always associated with excessive consumption of vitamin D supplements. It is very rare to develop high levels from sunlight exposure or dietary choices alone. High levels can lead to excess calcium buildup in the blood.

↓ If Low

Low levels can reflect limited sun exposure, inadequate dietary intake, or poor absorption in the digestive tract. It may also point to conditions affecting how your liver or kidneys process the vitamin into its active forms.

Symptoms associated with abnormal 25-OH vitamin D

↑ High Symptoms

  • Nausea, vomiting, or poor appetite
  • Frequent urination and excessive thirst
  • Constipation or abdominal pain
  • Muscle weakness or joint aches
  • Confusion, fatigue, or disorientation
  • Kidney stones or kidney discomfort

↓ Low Symptoms

  • Chronic fatigue or low energy levels
  • Bone pain, lower back discomfort, or joint aches
  • Muscle weakness or unexplained muscle cramps
  • Frequent illnesses or slow wound healing
  • Changes in mood, such as feelings of sadness
  • Hair thinning or hair loss
Common causes of abnormal 25-OH vitamin D

What can lower vitamin D?

The most frequent cause of low vitamin D is a lack of sun exposure, which is common in individuals who spend most of their time indoors, live in northern latitudes, or consistently wear high-SPF sunscreen. Having darker skin can also reduce the body's natural production of vitamin D from sunlight.

Other causes include malabsorption issues stemming from digestive conditions like celiac disease, Crohn's disease, or gastric bypass surgery. Obesity can also lower blood levels because vitamin D is fat-soluble and can become sequestered in body fat tissue. Additionally, certain kidney or liver disorders can hinder the body's ability to convert vitamin D into its measurable form.

What can raise vitamin D?

High vitamin D levels are almost entirely caused by taking high-dose over-the-counter or prescription supplements over an extended period. Because the body regulates how much vitamin D is produced from sunlight and how much is absorbed from standard whole foods, natural sources are unlikely to cause an overload.

Tests often ordered alongside 25-OH vitamin D

Vitamin D works closely with other nutrients and minerals. To get a complete overview of your bone health and metabolism, your provider may order:

  • Calcium — evaluates mineral balance, especially if vitamin D levels are unusually high or low
  • Parathyroid Hormone (PTH) — a hormone that works directly with vitamin D to maintain steady calcium levels
  • Phosphorus — another essential mineral that depends on vitamin D for proper absorption
  • Magnesium — required by the enzymes that metabolize and activate vitamin D in the body
What to do next
An insufficient or deficient vitamin D result is highly common and typically straightforward to address. Depending on your exact number, your lifestyle, and your medical background, your healthcare provider may suggest a targeted supplement plan or adjustments to your nutrition. Avoid starting high-dose therapeutic supplements without professional guidance, and always review your personal results alongside your calcium and doctor's recommendations.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Given my specific level, do you recommend a daily supplement, and if so, at what dosage?
  • 02Should I take Vitamin D3 or Vitamin D2, and should I take it alongside food?
  • 03Could any digestive conditions or other medications be affecting how I absorb nutrients?
  • 04Do I need to have my calcium or parathyroid hormone levels checked based on this result?
  • 05How long should I wait before we repeat this blood test to monitor my progress?
Frequently asked questions
Can I get enough vitamin D from food alone?
It can be challenging. Very few foods naturally contain high amounts of vitamin D. The best sources are fatty fish (like salmon and mackerel), fish liver oils, and egg yolks. Many people rely on fortified foods like milk, orange juice, and cereals, or supplements to meet their daily requirements.
What is the difference between Vitamin D2 and Vitamin D3?
Vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) is typically plant-derived and is often found in fortified foods and some high-dose prescription supplements. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is naturally produced by human skin in response to sunlight and is found in animal-sourced foods. Studies suggest D3 may be slightly more effective at raising blood levels long-term.
Does sunscreen prevent my body from making vitamin D?
Yes, sunscreen with a high SPF blocks the UV rays required for your skin to synthesize vitamin D. However, dermatologists generally recommend regular sunscreen use to protect skin health, as safe nutritional supplements and dietary sources can effectively maintain optimal vitamin D levels without UV damage.
Is a 25-hydroxy vitamin D test different from a 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D test?
Yes. The 25-hydroxy test measures your body's main storage reserves and is the standard test for evaluating deficiency. The 1,25-dihydroxy test measures the fully activated hormone form processed by the kidneys; it changes quickly and is typically only ordered for specific kidney or parathyroid conditions.
Do I need to fast before a 25-OH vitamin D blood test?
No fasting is required for this test. You can eat and drink normally beforehand, and it's often drawn alongside other routine bloodwork such as a metabolic panel.
