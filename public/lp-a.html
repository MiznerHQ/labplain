What does your
Lipoprotein(a) result mean?

Lipids & Heart
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

Lipoprotein(a)—often written as Lp(a) and pronounced "LP-little-A"—is a specific type of particle that carries cholesterol through your bloodstream. It is structurally very similar to LDL cholesterol (the so-called "bad" cholesterol), but it contains an extra sticky protein called apolipoprotein(a).

Because of this unique, sticky structure, high amounts of Lp(a) can collect more easily in the walls of your blood vessels. This buildup may contribute to narrow arteries, increase the likelihood of blood clots, and change the way your blood vessels respond to inflammation.

Unlike standard cholesterol markers that shift up and down based on your diet and activity levels, your Lp(a) concentration is primarily determined by your genetics. Doctors usually order this test once in a lifetime to uncover hidden risk factors for cardiovascular issues, especially if you have a family history of early heart disease.

Normal reference range
Desirable: Below 30 mg/dL  |  Below 75 nmol/L

Lipoprotein(a) measurements depend entirely on the specific method the laboratory uses. Some labs report findings in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL), which measures total mass, while others report in nanomoles per liter (nmol/L), which counts the individual particles. These units cannot be converted with a simple mathematical formula.

Because there is no universally standard conversion between units, you must compare your numeric value to the exact reference interval displayed directly on your medical report.

🧬 A Lifetime Marker

Your Lp(a) levels are highly stable and are largely established by the time you are one to two years old. They generally remain constant throughout adulthood, meaning lifestyle adjustments or routine physical variations will not noticeably alter this specific number.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

Elevated levels are often associated with an increased long-term risk of cardiovascular disease, heart attacks, strokes, or aortic valve narrowing. It highlights a genetic predisposition rather than a temporary health change.

↓ If Low

Lower values are typically considered ideal and are generally linked to standard baseline cardiovascular health. Low levels do not indicate any known medical disorders or negative health concerns.

Symptoms associated with abnormal lipoprotein(a)

↑ High Lp(a) Realities

  • No direct, noticeable daily symptoms
  • May be discovered after early heart issues
  • Can coexist with a family history of stroke
  • Often paired with stubborn LDL levels
  • May accompany early poor circulation

↓ Low Lp(a) Realities

  • Does not cause physical symptoms
  • Associated with lower baseline vascular stress
  • Considered a favorable clinical finding
  • Requires no medical management
Common causes of abnormal lipoprotein(a)

What causes high Lipoprotein(a)?

The main driver behind elevated Lipoprotein(a) is your inherited DNA. Variations in the LPA gene determine exactly how much of this protein your liver manufactures. Because this tracking metric is bound to genetics, standard heart-healthy dietary changes, weight loss, or typical exercise regimens do not significantly reduce it.

In certain scenarios, secondary health changes might influence the value. Conditions like severe kidney disease, a severely underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), or the natural drop in estrogen during menopause may sometimes be associated with a noticeable rise in circulating Lp(a).

What causes low Lipoprotein(a)?

Low or virtually undetectable levels of Lipoprotein(a) are entirely normal and stem from inheriting gene variants that naturally produce very little of this specific protein structure. Having low levels is common across many populations and is considered protective for blood vessel health.

Tests often ordered alongside Lipoprotein(a)

Lp(a) is interpreted alongside a comprehensive cardiovascular profile to map out overall systemic risk. Your doctor might evaluate:

  • Lipid Panel — to evaluate total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and baseline triglycerides
  • Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) — measures the total number of plaque-forming particles in your circulation
  • High-Sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) — tracks hidden signs of blood vessel inflammation
  • Fasting Glucose / HbA1c — checks for metabolic markers that impact overall vascular health
  • Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) Scan — an imaging test to check for physical plaque buildup in the heart arteries
What to do next
If your test reveals high Lipoprotein(a), remember that a single marker does not guarantee future heart issues. Because your background level is genetic, medical strategies focus heavily on aggressively managing all other cardiovascular risks. This means optimizing your standard LDL cholesterol, keeping blood pressure in check, maintaining steady blood sugar, and keeping an eye on your overall vascular wellness with your doctor.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Does this level of Lipoprotein(a) change how strictly we should manage my standard LDL cholesterol?
  • 02Should my siblings, parents, or children have their Lipoprotein(a) tested based on my result?
  • 03Are there advanced imaging tests, like a Coronary Artery Calcium scan, that would help check my physical arteries?
  • 04Given my overall profile, what are my target numbers for blood pressure and standard lipids?
  • 05Do you recommend any specific medical or therapeutic interventions to lower my systemic vascular risks?
Frequently asked questions
Can statins lower high Lipoprotein(a)?
Standard statin medications are highly effective at lowering routine LDL cholesterol, but they do not reduce Lipoprotein(a). In fact, statins can occasionally cause a slight increase in Lp(a) levels. However, doctors often still prescribe statins to individuals with high Lp(a) because lowering overall LDL cholesterol is a vital step in reducing total cardiovascular risk.
How often do I need to get my Lipoprotein(a) tested?
For most individuals, testing once in a lifetime is sufficient because levels are genetically predetermined and remain steady. Repeat testing is usually only considered if you are entering a new phase of health, such as menopause, or if you are undergoing specialized therapeutic clinical trials specifically targeting Lp(a).
Is Lipoprotein(a) the same thing as routine LDL cholesterol?
No. While an Lp(a) particle looks very similar to an LDL particle and carries cholesterol inside it, the added apolipoprotein(a) protein makes it distinct. Standard lipid panels measure total LDL cholesterol but bundle your Lp(a) content into that number without separating it out. This specific test isolates it.
Can diet and exercise reduce high Lp(a) levels?
No, traditional lifestyle changes do not significantly lower your baseline Lipoprotein(a) level due to its genetic nature. However, a balanced diet and regular exercise remain critical because they improve your blood pressure, metabolic health, and other lipid fractions, neutralizing overall heart risks.
