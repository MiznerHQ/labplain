What does your
Cortisol (PM Draw) result mean?

Hormones
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Always discuss your specific laboratory results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

The Cortisol (PM Draw) test measures the amount of cortisol in your blood during the late afternoon or early evening. Cortisol is your body's main stress hormone, produced by the adrenal glands sitting on top of your kidneys.

Under normal conditions, cortisol levels naturally follow a daily rhythm (diurnal rhythm). They peak early in the morning to help you wake up and progressively decline throughout the day, hitting their lowest points late in the evening and during early sleep.

Normal reference range
Typically less than 10 mcg/dL (Late Afternoon / Evening Draw)

Note: Reference ranges vary widely based on the exact time of collection (e.g., 4:00 PM vs. 8:00 PM) and individual lab standards. Always evaluate your result against the baseline range provided on your specific lab report.

What your result might indicate

Because your body expects cortisol levels to be drops-in-the-bucket low by evening, checking it later in the day helps doctors evaluate if your natural daily clock is functioning properly or if your body is producing too much hormone at night.

↑ If High (Elevated PM)

Cortisol levels that remain elevated in the afternoon or evening may indicate an overproduction of cortisol or a disruption in your natural hormonal curve. This is often seen in prolonged psychological stress, sleep disorders, or conditions like Cushing's syndrome.

↓ If Low (Suppressed PM)

Since cortisol is expected to be quite low in the PM, a very low afternoon value may be entirely normal. However, if paired with a low morning result, it could point toward overall adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) or prolonged steroid medication usage.

Associated Signs & Symptoms

If your PM cortisol is outside expected trends, you may experience symptoms related to altered metabolism, blood pressure regulation, and immune pathways:

For High PM Levels: Unexplained weight gain (especially around the abdomen and face), sleep disturbances/insomnia, high blood pressure, or muscle weakness.
For Low PM Levels: Chronic fatigue, unexplained weight loss, dizziness or lightheadedness when standing up, or salt cravings.
Common Causes of Out-of-Range Results

Potential causes of elevated PM cortisol include:

  • Chronic Stress: Mental, physical, or emotional strain can keep adrenal production running hot into evening hours.
  • Poor Sleep or Shift Work: Alterations in your normal wake-sleep cycle can shift your hormonal peaks out of position.
  • Cushing's Syndrome: A condition where the body produces excess cortisol due to an adrenal issue or a small growth in the pituitary gland.
  • Certain Medications: Oral contraceptives or steroid medications can artificially skew measured blood levels.

Potential causes of abnormally low baseline cortisol include:

  • Adrenal Insufficiency: Inability of the adrenal glands to generate enough hormone (Addison's disease).
  • Abrupt Steroid Discontinuation: Stopping long-term glucocorticoid medications (like prednisone) too quickly can temporarily put adrenal production to sleep.

What to do next
Because cortisol fluctuates dynamically, it is vital to record the exact time your blood was collected. Compare this timestamp directly with your laboratory's evening guidelines. Avoid intense exercise, caffeine, or major stressors immediately before a PM collection as they can cause acute, temporary spikes.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • Does this afternoon value align with the exact time my blood was drawn?
  • Do we need to couple this with a morning blood draw or a 24-hour collection to view my complete daily cycle?
  • Could any of my current supplements, prescriptions, or lifestyle stressors be impacting this evening reading?
  • Based on my symptoms, do you recommend any dynamic hormone tracking tests?
Frequently Asked Questions

Why would my doctor check my cortisol in the afternoon instead of the morning?

A morning test checks your peak capability, while an afternoon or evening test evaluates if your cortisol is dropping appropriately as the day closes. Failing to drop in the evening is a primary indicator for specific endocrine conditions.

Can stress right before the needle stick change my results?

Yes. Acute anxiety or stress from the blood draw itself can trigger a brief output of cortisol from the adrenal glands, potentially causing a mild artificial elevation.

Is a saliva test or a blood test better for evening cortisol?

Both have merits. Blood tests measure total cortisol (bound and free), while late-night saliva tests are frequently used because they are simple to perform at home right before bed without the stress of traveling to a clinic.
