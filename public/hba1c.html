What does your
HbA1c result mean?

Diabetes & Metabolism
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

The Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) test measures the average amount of glucose (blood sugar) that has been attached to your red blood cells over the past 2 to 3 months. Hemoglobin is a protein inside your red blood cells that carries oxygen, and when glucose builds up in your bloodstream, it naturally binds to this protein through a process called glycation.

Because red blood cells live for about 120 days, analyzing the percentage of glycated hemoglobin gives healthcare providers a reliable picture of your long-term blood sugar trends. Unlike a standard finger-prick blood glucose test, which captures your sugar levels at one precise second, the A1c smooths out daily spikes and drops caused by your last meal or recent exercise.

Doctors order an HbA1c test to screen for prediabetes, diagnose type 1 or type 2 diabetes, or monitor blood sugar management in individuals who have already been diagnosed with these metabolic conditions.

Normal reference range
Normal: Under 5.7%  |  Prediabetes: 5.7%–6.4%  |  Diabetes: 6.5% or higher

The HbA1c result is reported as a percentage of your total hemoglobin. If your result is between 5.7% and 6.4%, it indicates prediabetes, meaning your blood sugar levels are higher than typical but not yet high enough to be classified as diabetes. A level of 6.5% or higher on two separate tests generally indicates diabetes.

For individuals managing existing diabetes, individual targets vary. Many clinical guidelines suggest aiming for an HbA1c below 7.0%, though your specific target should be customized by your physician.

⏰ Why timing matters

Unlike fasting glucose tests, you do not need to fast before an HbA1c test. You can eat and drink normally, and the blood sample can be drawn at any time of day. Because it reflects a multi-month average, a single day of healthy eating or a brief sugar spike right before the test will not significantly alter your result.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

An elevated HbA1c level typically indicates insulin resistance, prediabetes, or diabetes. It suggests that your body is having difficulty moving glucose out of your bloodstream and into your cells for energy, leaving higher averages of sugar behind.

↓ If Low

An unusually low HbA1c (often below 4.0%) is less common. It may be associated with frequent episodes of low blood sugar (hypoglycemia), or conditions that alter red blood cell life cycles, such as certain types of anemia or recent blood loss.

Symptoms associated with abnormal HbA1c

↑ High HbA1c Symptoms

  • Increased thirst and dry mouth
  • Frequent urination, especially at night
  • Unexplained fatigue or low energy
  • Blurry or hazy vision
  • Slow-healing cuts, sores, or bruises
  • Unintended weight loss
  • Frequent skin or yeast infections

↓ Low HbA1c Symptoms

  • Shakiness, trembling, or jitteriness
  • Sweating, chills, or clamminess
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness
  • Rapid or irregular heartbeat
  • Sudden, intense hunger
  • Irritability, anxiety, or mood changes
  • Headaches or confusion
Common causes of abnormal HbA1c

What can raise HbA1c?

The most common underlying factor behind an elevated HbA1c is a reduced cellular response to insulin, often referred to as insulin resistance. This is strongly associated with lifestyle factors such as a sedentary routine, long-term stress, poor sleep hygiene, and diets high in refined carbohydrates and ultra-processed foods. Chronic inflammation, obesity, and genetic predispositions also play primary roles in rising levels.

Additionally, certain non-metabolic factors can artificially elevate an HbA1c reading. Conditions that prolong the life of red blood cells—such as iron deficiency anemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, or recent splenectomy—can lead to higher results because the cells have been exposed to circulating sugars for a longer period.

What can lower HbA1c?

In individuals managing diabetes, a low or rapidly falling HbA1c often reflects aggressive medication dosages, such as excessive insulin or sulfonylureas, which can lead to unsafe drops in blood sugar. Outside of medication, significant dietary overhauls, weight loss, and consistent physical activity naturally lower the average.

Physiologically, anything that shortens the lifespan of your red blood cells can skew the test lower, as the cells do not live long enough to collect a normal amount of glucose. This is often seen in conditions like sickle cell anemia, thalassemia, chronic kidney disease, recent blood transfusions, or blood loss.

Tests often ordered alongside HbA1c

HbA1c provides a long-term overview, but doctors often pair it with other assessments to build a complete diagnostic roadmap:

  • Fasting Blood Glucose — captures your exact blood sugar level at the moment of the draw after fasting for 8+ hours.
  • Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) — tracks how your body handles a specific load of sugar over a two-hour window.
  • Lipid Panel — measures cholesterol and triglycerides, as metabolic issues often impact cardiovascular health.
  • Estimated Average Glucose (eAG) — a calculated number that converts your HbA1c percentage into a daily mg/dL unit.
  • Urinary Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (UACR) — a simple urine test used to screen for early signs of kidney strain related to blood sugar imbalances.
What to do next
A single elevated HbA1c result is an important indicator, but it does not tell the entire story of your metabolic health. Because various underlying conditions can shift red blood cell lifespans, your doctor will look at this result alongside your daily symptoms, physical exams, and potentially follow-up tests before finalizing a diagnosis. Avoid making sudden, extreme changes to your routine or medications based on this single percentage without consulting your healthcare provider first.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Does my HbA1c result place me in the normal, prediabetes, or diabetes category?
  • 02Could any underlying factors, like anemia or iron levels, be skewing my percentage?
  • 03Do you recommend adjusting my diet and exercise, or is it time to explore medication options?
  • 04What is my personalized target HbA1c goal over the next three to six months?
  • 05How frequently should we re-test my HbA1c to monitor my progress?
  • 06Are there other markers, like fasting insulin or a lipid profile, we should look at together?
Frequently asked questions
Can a high-sugar meal the night before the test alter my HbA1c?
No. Because the HbA1c test reflects an average of your blood sugar over a 90-day window, a single meal or short-term indulgence will not significantly shift the overall result. It is your consistent, day-to-day baseline that shapes this percentage.
Can you reverse a high HbA1c level?
If your results point to prediabetes or early-stage type 2 diabetes, targeted lifestyle adjustments—such as increasing physical activity, shifts in dietary patterns, stress reduction, and improving sleep quality—can frequently return your HbA1c to a normal reference range.
How often should I get my HbA1c checked?
For individuals with stable blood sugar within a normal range, an annual check is typical. If you are diagnosed with prediabetes or are actively managing diabetes with therapy adjustments, your doctor will likely recommend a check every 3 to 6 months.
What is the difference between HbA1c and a standard glucose test?
A standard glucose test shows your blood sugar level at one exact moment, which can fluctuate wildly depending on stress, food, or activity. The HbA1c test measures the percentage of sugar permanently attached to your red blood cells, providing a steady multi-month average.
Can pregnancy affect my HbA1c results?
Yes. Pregnancy can change red blood cell turnover rates, meaning HbA1c might not accurately reflect true blood sugar trends. Instead, healthcare providers typically rely on oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT) to screen for gestational diabetes.
