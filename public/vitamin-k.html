What does your
Vitamin K result mean?

Vitamins & Nutrition
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

A vitamin K blood test measures the amount of phylloquinone (vitamin K1) circulating in your blood. Vitamin K is a fat-soluble nutrient that plays an essential role in your body's ability to form blood clots and manage calcium distribution to keep bones strong.

Without sufficient vitamin K, your liver cannot produce the vital clotting factors needed to stop bleeding from cuts, injuries, or internal tissues. Your body acquires vitamin K through green leafy vegetables (as vitamin K1) and relies on beneficial gut bacteria to produce secondary variations (vitamin K2).

Doctors typically order this test if you exhibit unexplained bleeding or easy bruising, suffer from a malabsorption condition like celiac disease, or require monitoring during long-term antibiotic therapy.

Normal reference range
Adult Standard: 0.1–2.2 ng/mL

Because vitamin K levels drop rapidly if you fast or alter your short-term diet, a standard reference range can fluctuate based on recent meals. Your lab report may display slightly different target bands depending on the testing technique utilized.

A single blood test for vitamin K can sometimes show normal levels even if tissue levels are low. Healthcare providers frequently rely on indirect clotting speed metrics alongside this specific numbers check.

⏰ Fasting Requirements

This test usually requires a 12-hour fast from food and beverages (except water) before your blood sample is collected. Consuming a meal rich in green leaves right before the test can artificially spike your values, masking an underlying issue.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

Elevated blood levels of vitamin K are uncommon through dietary sources alone. High values are frequently associated with heavy therapeutic supplementation or targeted clinical dosing. High dietary intake generally does not spark toxicity in healthy adults.

↓ If Low

Low vitamin K values may reflect poor nutritional intake, severe digestive malabsorption, or extended antibiotic regimens that deplete gut flora. Low levels can compromise blood clotting stability and warrant structured medical review.

Symptoms associated with abnormal vitamin K

↑ High Vitamin K Symptoms

  • Symptoms are rarely seen from high food intake
  • Potential interference with anticoagulant pills
  • Flushing or sweating (with rapid prescription injections)
  • Jaundice or anemia (exclusively in infants given synthetic forms)

↓ Low Vitamin K Symptoms

  • Easy bruising from minor bumps
  • Frequent, unexplained nosebleeds
  • Bleeding gums during routine brushing
  • Heavy or prolonged menstrual periods
  • Oozing from small cuts or injection sites
  • Dark, tarry stools or blood in the urine
Common causes of abnormal vitamin K

What can raise vitamin K?

True toxicity or naturally occurring high levels are exceedingly rare because the body clears this vitamin efficiently. When high values show up on a lab report, the primary catalyst is typically intentional high-dose over-supplementation or medical prescriptions intended to reverse certain bleeding concerns.

Consuming vast helpings of kale, spinach, or broccoli can trigger brief elevations in blood tracking, but these do not typically match the critical levels caused by miscalculated vitamin supplement routines.

What can lower vitamin K?

A true deficiency can show up if your diet lacks green vegetables, though it is frequently caused by a digestive tract problem. Conditions that alter how your body handles dietary fats—such as celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, Crohn's disease, chronic pancreatitis, or weight-loss surgeries—can prevent proper vitamin absorption.

Extended use of broad-spectrum antibiotics can lower levels by clearing out the helpful intestinal bacteria that naturally synthesize vitamin K forms. Additionally, certain medications like bile acid sequestrants can block normal processing pathways.

Tests often ordered alongside vitamin K

Vitamin K is regularly interpreted alongside broader functional checks to verify how well your blood actually clots:

  • Prothrombin Time (PT / INR) — A benchmark assessment measuring the exact seconds it takes for your blood sample to form a clot.
  • Partial Thromboplastin Time (PTT) — Another clotting screen used to evaluate secondary clotting pathways.
  • Vitamin D and Calcium — Checked because vitamin K coordinates with these nutrients to build bone structures.
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC) — Assesses red blood cells and platelets to rule out mechanical bleeding causes.
  • Liver Function Panel — Evaluates liver health, where vitamin K dependent clotting agents are built.
What to do next
An isolated out-of-range vitamin K value does not paint a full clinical picture on its own. Your healthcare provider will read your lab result alongside your current symptoms, physical exam, and standard clotting markers before mapping out a strategy. Avoid initiating high-dose vitamins independently, as altering these levels can change how your cardiovascular and clotting mechanisms run.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Does my vitamin K level correspond with my current bruising or bleeding tendencies?
  • 02Could an undiagnosed digestive or gut absorption issue be causing this result?
  • 03Do my companion clotting metrics (like PT/INR) confirm a functional deficiency?
  • 04Are any of my current medications, over-the-counter supplements, or teas skewing this test?
  • 05Is it safer for me to correct this via specific dietary adjustments, or do I require structured supplementation?
  • 06How long should I wait before we run a follow-up test to verify my levels have stabilized?
Frequently asked questions
Can a vitamin K test show if my blood thinners are working?
Not directly. If you take traditional blood thinners like warfarin (Coumadin), doctors track your clotting speed using a PT/INR test rather than measuring vitamin K directly. Warfarin works by reducing vitamin K activity, so monitoring how fast your blood clots is the standard way to check your dose.
What foods have the highest impact on vitamin K levels?
Dark, green leafy items pack the highest concentration. Kale, collard greens, spinach, turnip greens, Swiss chard, and Brussels sprouts are rich sources. Consuming steady, predictable portions of these foods is generally recommended, especially if you are taking medications that interact with vitamin K.
Why do newborn babies receive a vitamin K shot at birth?
Infants are born with very low levels of vitamin K because the nutrient does not cross the placenta well, and their clean intestines lack the bacteria needed to manufacture it. The preventative injection helps protect newborns from a rare but serious bleeding condition called Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB).
What is the difference between vitamin K1 and vitamin K2?
Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) is found mostly in plants and greens, serving as the primary driver for liver clotting functions. Vitamin K2 (menaquinone) is created by bacteria, found in fermented foods or animal products, and specializes in directing calcium into your bones instead of your arteries. Standard blood tests primarily track K1.
