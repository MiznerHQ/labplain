What does your
Sed Rate (ESR) result mean?

Inflammation
⚠ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

Sed Rate, short for erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), is a common blood test used to check for inflammatory activity in your body. It is an indirect measure of inflammation, tracking how quickly your red blood cells (erythrocytes) settle at the bottom of a tall, thin test tube over the course of one hour.

Normally, red blood cells settle relatively slowly. However, when there is active inflammation in your body, your liver and immune system produce higher levels of proteins (like fibrinogen). These proteins cause the red blood cells to stick together, forming dense clumps known as "rouleaux." Because these clumps are heavier than individual cells, they sink to the bottom of the tube much faster.

Doctors frequently order a sed rate test when evaluating unexplained fevers, joint pain, muscle aches, or persistent headaches. It is heavily utilized to detect and monitor conditions like corporate infections, cancers, and autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, or giant cell arteritis.

Normal reference range
Men < 50 years: Less than 15 mm/hr  |  Men > 50 years: Less than 20 mm/hr
Women < 50 years: Less than 20 mm/hr  |  Women > 50 years: Less than 30 mm/hr

Sed rate is measured in millimeters per hour (mm/hr). Normal values vary naturally based on both age and sex. Women typically have higher baseline values than men, and sed rates naturally rise as you grow older.

Reference ranges can also vary slightly depending on the specific testing lab. Always refer directly to the exact range printed on your physical or digital lab report.

🧪 A non-specific indicator

The sed rate test is "non-specific," meaning it can alert your doctor that inflammation is happening somewhere in your body, but it cannot pinpoint the exact location or the precise cause. Think of it like a check engine light for your immune health.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

An elevated sed rate indicates active inflammation. This may stem from systemic infections, tissue injuries, or autoimmune diseases. Extremely high results (over 100 mm/hr) often indicate serious conditions like bone infections, giant cell arteritis, or certain cancers like multiple myeloma.

↓ If Low

A low sed rate means red blood cells sink slower than normal. This is usually not a cause for medical concern, but it can sometimes point to blood disorders that alter cell counts or thickness (like polycythemia or sickle cell disease), severe liver issues, or congestive heart failure.

Symptoms associated with abnormal sed rate

↑ High Sed Rate Symptoms

  • Stiff, swollen, or painful joints
  • Persistent, unexplained headaches
  • Pain in the neck, shoulders, or hips
  • Unexplained fever or chills
  • Unexpected weight loss
  • Loss of appetite
  • Digestive issues or ongoing diarrhea

↓ Low Sed Rate Symptoms

  • Severe fatigue or weakness
  • Shortness of breath during exertion
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness
  • Swelling in the legs, ankles, or feet
  • Frequent headaches or visual shifts
  • Symptoms matching an underlying blood disorder
Common causes of abnormal sed rate

What can raise sed rate?

The most common cause of an elevated sed rate is an inflammatory response triggered by your immune system. Autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, polymyalgia rheumatica, and giant cell (temporal) arteritis are prime culprits. Infections throughout the body—including bone infections, heart valve infections (endocarditis), and respiratory illnesses like pneumonia—will also drive up your numbers.

Other conditions that elevate sed rate include kidney disease, thyroid disorders, anemia, and certain blood-related cancers like lymphoma or multiple myeloma. Non-medical factors can also temporarily raise your sed rate, including pregnancy, menstruation, regular alcohol usage, advanced age, and obesity.

What can lower sed rate?

A low sed rate typically happens when blood properties prevent cells from clumping or settling normally. This includes polycythemia (an excessive baseline number of red blood cells making blood too thick), leukocytosis (an exceptionally high white blood cell count), or sickle cell disease, where abnormal cell shapes alter settling mechanics.

It can also be driven by low plasma protein counts caused by severe underlying liver or kidney failure, as well as congestive heart failure. Highly elevated blood sugar levels can also cause a falsely lowered sed rate reading.

Tests often ordered alongside sed rate

Because sed rate doesn't name a specific disease on its own, it is rarely interpreted in a vacuum. Your doctor may order:

  • C-Reactive Protein (CRP) — Another key blood test measuring inflammation, which responds quicker to bodily changes than a sed rate.
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC) — Checks your red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets to evaluate for infection or anemia.
  • Rheumatoid Factor (RF) & Anti-CCP — Diagnostic markers specifically targeted toward screening for rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Antinuclear Antibody (ANA) — A screening panel used to evaluate for lupus and related systemic autoimmune conditions.
  • Comprehensive Metabolic Panel (CMP) — Evaluates your liver and kidney function, as well as blood glucose levels.
What to do next
An isolated out-of-range sed rate reading is just one puzzle piece. Because your sed rate shifts in response to temporary bugs, everyday aging, minor injuries, or structural lifestyle elements, your doctor will evaluate your scores alongside your real-world symptoms and broader health context. Do not jump to alarming conclusions over an elevated number—schedule a review to investigate the overall medical layout safely.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Does my elevated sed rate point to a temporary issue, or does it look like a chronic inflammatory condition?
  • 02How well do my current symptoms align with this specific level of inflammation?
  • 03Should we order a C-Reactive Protein (CRP) test or an ANA panel to clarify these findings?
  • 04Could any of my current daily medications, habits, or supplements be skewing this result?
  • 05Based on my history, would a referral to a rheumatologist or an infectious disease specialist be appropriate?
  • 06When should we run a repeat sed rate test to see if the inflammation is subsiding?
Frequently asked questions
Can anxiety or emotional stress cause a high sed rate?
Direct psychological stress or minor anxiety has not shown a strong, reliable link to elevated sed rate values in research. While stress influences cortisol and blood pressure, a high sed rate is specifically a physical reflection of blood proteins and active inflammatory or immune pathways.
What is the main difference between a Sed Rate (ESR) and a CRP test?
Both tests measure systemic inflammation, but they track different things. CRP measures a specific liver protein that spikes and clears rapidly during acute inflammation. Sed rate tracks how fast red blood cells settle, which responds more slowly over a period of days or weeks, making it useful for evaluating longer-term chronic trends.
Can my sed rate drop or return to normal naturally?
Yes. If your elevated sed rate was driven by a passing virus, minor physical injury, or temporary bacterial infection, your values will gradually descend back into your normal baseline range as your immune system recovers and blood protein compositions rebalance.
Is a sed rate test useful for diagnosing cancer?
It cannot diagnose cancer directly. While certain malignancies (such as multiple myeloma or lymphoma) can cause extremely high sed rates due to elevated blood proteins, many non-cancerous, routine conditions also spike the test. It serves as an alert system, not a definitive diagnostic tool.
