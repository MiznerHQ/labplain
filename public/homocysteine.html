What does your
Homocysteine result mean?

Cardiovascular & Vitamins
⚠️ Educational only. LabPlain does not provide medical advice or diagnosis. Always discuss your specific results with your healthcare provider.
What this test measures

Homocysteine is a common amino acid (a building block of protein) found in your blood. You mostly get it from eating meat. In a healthy body, homocysteine is quickly broken down and converted into other substances that your body needs.

Your body relies heavily on vitamins—specifically B12, B6, and folate (B9)—to clear homocysteine out of your bloodstream. If your body lacks these vitamins, or if it has trouble processing them, homocysteine can build up to higher levels.

Doctors typically order this test if they suspect a vitamin deficiency, if you have signs of a rare genetic condition called homocystinuria, or sometimes to help assess your overall risk of cardiovascular conditions like heart disease or strokes.

Normal reference range
Adult Standard: Less than 15.0 mcmol/L

An optimal level is often considered to be below 10.0 mcmol/L by many clinicians, while levels between 15.0 and 30.0 mcmol/L are classified as mildly elevated. High levels are sometimes categorized as moderate (30 to 100 mcmol/L) or severe (greater than 100 mcmol/L).

Reference ranges vary slightly depending on the lab, your age, and your biological sex. Always compare your result directly to the reference range listed on your final lab report.

⏰ Why fasting matters

You may be asked to fast (not eat or drink anything except water) for 10 to 12 hours before this blood draw. Eating a meal rich in protein right before the test can temporarily raise your homocysteine levels and alter your results.

What your result might indicate
↑ If High

Elevated homocysteine (hyperhomocysteinemia) frequently points to a lack of B vitamins (B12, B6, or folate). It may also be associated with kidney disease, thyroid changes, certain lifestyle habits, or inherited genetic variants like MTHFR.

↓ If Low

Low homocysteine is relatively uncommon and generally not considered a clinical concern. It may occasionally be seen with high intakes of specific vitamin supplements, or it may simply reflect your individual baseline metabolism.

Symptoms associated with abnormal homocysteine

↑ High Homocysteine Symptoms

  • Fatigue or weakness (if tied to B-vitamin deficiency)
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness
  • Numbness or tingling in hands or feet
  • Pale skin or a sore, red tongue
  • Mood changes or trouble concentrating
  • Shortness of breath during light exertion

↓ Low Homocysteine Symptoms

  • Typically causes no symptoms at all
  • Generally discovered incidentally on a lab panel
  • Not linked to any standard clinical syndrome
Common causes of abnormal homocysteine

What can raise homocysteine?

The most common culprit behind high homocysteine is a vitamin B12, B6, or folate deficiency. Without enough of these vitamins, your body cannot efficiently dismantle homocysteine, causing it to pool in the blood. Kidney disease can also raise levels because the kidneys are responsible for filtering out excess amino acids.

Other factors include lifestyle choices like regular smoking or high alcohol intake, older age, and hypothyroid function. Rare genetic mutations, such as variants in the MTHFR gene, can also impair how your body handles folate and lead to higher baselines.

What can lower homocysteine?

Low levels are rarely a focal point in medicine. They may occur if you are taking high doses of B-vitamin supplements or if your liver is synthesizing proteins at a different rate. In almost all cases, a low value is considered perfectly safe.

Tests often ordered alongside homocysteine

To pinpoint the exact reason behind your homocysteine level, your provider might request:

  • Vitamin B12 & Folate — directly verifies whether a vitamin deficiency is driving the elevation
  • Methylmalonic Acid (MMA) — a highly specific test used to confirm a true vitamin B12 deficiency
  • Complete Blood Count (CBC) — looks for enlarged red blood cells, which can happen with low B12 or folate
  • Creatinine & BUN — evaluates kidney health to ensure your body is filtering waste properly
  • Lipid Panel — checks cholesterol levels to build a broader picture of your cardiovascular health
What to do next
An elevated homocysteine level is a helpful clue, but it isn't an immediate emergency or a standalone diagnosis. Because it is highly sensitive to your diet and your vitamin intake, it can often be safely managed. Do not start high-dose vitamin protocols on your own, as balance matters. Share the report with your doctor so they can cross-reference it with your symptoms and overall medical history.
Questions to ask your doctor
  • 01Could a lack of vitamin B12 or folate be the main reason my levels are high?
  • 02Should we run secondary testing, like an MMA test, to double-check my vitamin status?
  • 03Do you recommend I modify my diet or introduce a specific B-complex supplement?
  • 04Could my current medications or lifestyle choices be affecting this result?
  • 05In light of my personal health history, does this level change how we view my heart health?
Frequently asked questions
Does a high homocysteine level mean I will get heart disease?
No. While research indicates an association between high homocysteine and vascular changes, it is considered one risk factor among many—like blood pressure, smoking, and cholesterol. It does not act as absolute proof that a medical event will occur.
Can I lower my homocysteine naturally?
Yes, if the elevation is driven by diet. Consuming foods rich in folate (like leafy greens and beans) and vitamin B12 (like eggs, fish, and fortified cereals) often helps. Stopping smoking and moderating alcohol consumption can also support healthier levels.
What is the connection between MTHFR and homocysteine?
The MTHFR gene provides instructions for making an enzyme that processes folate. Certain common variations in this gene can make the enzyme less efficient, which may sometimes lead to higher homocysteine levels in the blood, especially if your folate intake is already low.
Can medications alter my homocysteine results?
Yes. Several common medications—including certain cholesterol-lowering drugs, drugs for diabetes like metformin, or acid-reflux medications that block vitamin absorption—can cause homocysteine to climb. Always share your complete medication list with your provider.
